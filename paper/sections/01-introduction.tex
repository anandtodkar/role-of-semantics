\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{The gap between a competent model and a competent operator}

The current wave of industrial AI rests on a simple observation: a large
language model that can read a manual, write a query and call a function is, in
principle, a general-purpose operator's assistant. Such demonstrations are
persuasive, whether summarising a shift log, explaining an alarm, drafting the
structured text for a new interlock, or proposing a setpoint. Robust deployments
have been slower to follow. The failures are rarely failures of fluency, or even
of reasoning; they are failures of \emph{reference}: the model cannot determine
which physical entity the words denote.

Consider a request that a human operator answers immediately: \emph{``what is
the outlet pressure of the Line~1 filler?''} In the reference plant used
throughout this paper, five distinct tags carry the description string
``Outlet pressure''. Two of them are on fillers, on different production lines,
commissioned nine years apart; one is calibrated in \texttt{bar} and the other
in \texttt{psi}. A sixth tag, on the same filler, is the pressure
\emph{setpoint} rather than the measurement, and is writable where the other is
not. A model given a historian export sees six rows of near-identical English text
and a column of numbers. It will produce an answer, and may produce the correct
one, because the tag mnemonics encode the plant hierarchy for a reader who knows
the convention. Nothing in the representation, however, makes the answer
\emph{checkable}, and nothing prevents the same model, within the same session,
from proposing a write to the read-only measurement rather than the setpoint, in
the wrong unit, while the machine is in a state that forbids the change.

This is the problem that semantic technologies address, and industrial
automation has addressed it over some three decades: \opcua{} information models,
the Asset Administration Shell, ECLASS property dictionaries, AutomationML
engineering exchange, ISA-95 equipment hierarchies, PackML state machines and the
Module Type Package. From the web side, the relevant technologies include RDF,
OWL, SHACL, SPARQL, QUDT, SOSA/SSN and a range of domain ontologies. Their
original motivation was machine-to-machine interoperability rather than machine
comprehension. This paper asks whether that investment now yields a second
benefit, by examining how much of what a language agent needs is already
standardised, which elements are missing, and what each contributes to
measurable accuracy.

\subsection{A combined survey and empirical study}

Surveys of industrial semantic technology exist, as does a fast-growing
literature on grounding language models with knowledge graphs, but the two rarely
connect. Standards surveys evaluate technologies against interoperability
requirements such as coverage, tool support and conformance, which are not the
criteria that matter for an agent. Knowledge-graph--RAG work evaluates on
open-domain question answering over encyclopaedic graphs, in which there are no
units, no interlocks, no read-only variables and no operational consequence to an
incorrect answer.

This paper therefore combines two contributions. First
(\S\ref{sec:layer0}--\S\ref{sec:layer4}) it surveys the field through a lens built
for agents: a ten-criterion
\emph{agent-usability framework} (\S\ref{sec:framework}) that asks of every
technology what it lets an agent \emph{resolve}, \emph{type}, \emph{convert},
\emph{traverse}, \emph{do}, \emph{check}, \emph{trust}, \emph{query},
\emph{afford} and \emph{deploy}. Second (\S\ref{sec:design}--\S\ref{sec:results})
it builds a reproducible testbed in which those criteria are ablated one layer at
a time, and measures the consequences.

The central experiments deliberately avoid benchmarking a particular model.
Model rankings expire within months, and an accuracy difference measured on one
model is weakly informative about the representation, because the reader is not
held constant. The study instead measures properties of the representation
itself, and then confirms with a single dated real-model run (Experiment~E11) that
a contemporary model's accuracy does follow these properties. The representational
measures are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Grounding:} whether a fixed resolution algorithm maps a
  natural-language mention onto the correct tag, given only what the
  representation exposes.
  \item \textbf{Sufficiency ceiling:} whether the retrieved context contains
  \emph{every} fact the question requires. A perfect reader cannot exceed this
  bound, and a gap here cannot be closed by prompting, fine-tuning or scaling.
  \item \textbf{Guardrail efficacy:} for a corpus of faulty agent actions, the
  fraction that can be \emph{proved} wrong, and the fraction of legal actions
  wrongly rejected.
  \item \textbf{Action-space fidelity:} the proportion of the emittable call
  space that the representation eliminates, and whether it eliminates the correct
  part.
  \item \textbf{Context economy:} the token cost of the representation, the
  conditions under which a query is cheaper than filling the window, and whether a
  rich representation expends its budget on layers the question did not require.
  \item \textbf{Robustness:} the extent to which the un-grounded baseline's
  apparent competence derives from the tag naming convention, and the effect of a
  convention that is different, absent, or untruthful.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{C\arabic*.},leftmargin=2.6em]
  \item \textbf{A layered map of industrial semantics for agents.} We organise
  24 standards and vocabularies into five layers
  (L0 identification, L1 information models, L2 formal semantics, L3 domain
  ontologies, L4 dataspaces) and show that the layers are not interchangeable:
  each contributes a distinct and non-substitutable agent affordance
  (\S\ref{sec:framework}--\S\ref{sec:layer4}).

  \item \textbf{The agent-usability framework (AUF).} Ten criteria with a
  published four-point rubric (Appendix~\ref{app:rubric}), applied uniformly to
  every surveyed technology, yielding a comparison matrix
  (Table~\ref{tab:auf-matrix}) that makes the field's blind spots visible: unit
  semantics, behavioural semantics and constraint semantics are supported by far
  fewer standards than type semantics.

  \item \textbf{A reproducible industrial grounding benchmark.} A reference
  bottling plant with \NAssets{} assets and \NSignals{} signals, projected into
  seven interchange formats (RDF, \opcua{} NodeSet2, AAS~JSON, WoT~TD, NGSI-LD,
  JSON-LD and a historian CSV) from one declarative source, re-taggable under
  \NSchemes{} naming conventions, plus \NTasks{} agent tasks with declared
  evidence requirements and \NCases{} labelled proposed agent actions across
  twelve fault families (\S\ref{sec:design}).

  \item \textbf{Nine experiments quantifying the contribution of each semantic layer.}
  Grounding, sufficiency, serialisation economy, guardrail efficacy,
  action-space reduction, the accuracy ceiling, query-versus-stuffing,
  robustness to the tag naming convention, and the effect of intent-routed
  projection (\S\ref{sec:results}), together with a dated real-model sanity check
  (Experiment~E11) that confirms the ceilings predict a contemporary model's
  accuracy.

  \item \textbf{Ten findings and a design pattern.} Chief among them: a fully
  typed information model, which is the current state of practice, is a
  \emph{local optimum} that leaves the most dangerous failure modes undetectable
  (\S\ref{sec:discussion}); and constraint checking should be treated as a
  first-class deliverable of the semantic layer rather than a by-product.

  \item \textbf{An open artefact.} Every table, figure and number in this paper
  is regenerated by \texttt{python run\_experiments.py}, offline, deterministically,
  in under a minute (Appendix~\ref{app:artifacts}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Thesis and summary of findings}

Semantics is not a single capability, and the aspects that industry has
standardised most thoroughly are not those that agents require most.
Identification and typing, the mature part of the field, raise entity grounding
from \GrndTopOneCZero{} to \GrndTopOneCTwo{} and allow a validator to catch
invalid references, out-of-range values and permission violations. They
contribute little to the consequential failure modes: a command that is illegal
in the current machine state, a write that violates an interlock, a value that is
numerically correct in the wrong unit, and an answer that contradicts the
observation it claims to quote. These require unit ontologies, behavioural models,
explicit constraint models and provenance; adding them raises guardrail recall
from \RecallGTwo{} to \RecallGFour{} and the task ceiling from \CeilingCTwo{} to
\CeilingCFour{}. Two corollaries have practical importance: addressing grounding
by placing a larger, better-typed catalogue into a larger context window is
ineffective (Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling}b); and the competence that makes an
un-grounded pipeline appear adequate derives largely from the tag naming
convention, a form of semantics that cannot be audited (\S\ref{sec:e8}). A
contemporary model run through the same conditions confirms the pattern: its
accuracy climbs from \EllAccCZero{} to \EllAccCFour{} as the layers are added, and
exceeds the evidence ceiling only where the naming convention lets it guess
(\S\ref{sec:e11}).
